Do a Good Job With This

Put real effort into this one. Here's why.

This profile is the foundation for your whole research proposal. Over these three weeks you will build your proposal one piece at a time, and every piece builds on what you put in here. What actually matters is the process you go through to get there, step by step. You will dig up documents, track down real sources, and do the research yourself. If you skip the process and just try to get to a finished paper, you skip the part that was supposed to teach you anything.

This Rhetorical Situation Profile is one of the first steps in that process, and everything after it depends on it. If you don't do this well, the rest of it just sucks. It won't have the real context you need to build an actual scenario, because you won't have actually thought through who you're trying to convince.

Convincing someone isn't just handing them data. That's not how people actually work. You need the human element too: what's their point of view, what's their paradigm, why would they specifically care about this. That's why you have to pick a real, specific person instead of a made up "supervisor." If you don't know who that person actually is, you can't figure out how to convince them.

So take this seriously and fill it out for real. What you put in here is what everything after it gets built on.

One more thing. Nothing in this process hands you finished answers. The workbooks give you structure and keep you organized, but finding and reading the actual material is on you. Your instructor will read what you wrote, so write it like it matters.

STEP 1

Your Role (Author)

Who are you as the person making this case? A vague answer here makes everything after it harder to trust, so this section gets built with the same specificity as your supervisor's.

Doing This Well

Details are what make this section work. A vague role gives your supervisor no reason to take you seriously.

  • Name something you actually do, not just a title. "I work in the shop" tells nobody anything. "I run alignments on about ten vehicles a week" does.
  • Use numbers wherever you can: how many, how often, how long.
  • Say what you see that other people don't because of your role. That is your standing to bring this forward.
  • Include a real piece of your background that shapes how you show up at work.
Where this goes: this doesn't get its own section. It shapes how credible you sound and what you decide to include in the whole paper.

STEP 2

Your Supervisor (Audience)

Your supervisor cannot be invented. Pick a real person you actually know, a parent, a former or current manager, a coach, a teacher, and imagine them as your future boss in the field you're going into. You need to know them well enough to answer honestly, not guess.

Doing This Well

Details are what make this section work. If a stranger could not tell who this person is from what you wrote, it is not detailed enough.

  • Pick a real person you know well and write an actual memory: where you were, what they did, and what they said.
  • Include something they really said, in their words or close to it.
  • Tie your paradigm and bias explanations to one specific moment you saw. "She cares about safety" is thin. "She held up three jobs because a lift was leaking fluid" is detailed.
  • Connect their generation or background back to the memory you described, so it reads as one person, not a list of labels.
  • Say what they would ask first if you pitched them something, because that tells you what they care about most.
Where this goes: this doesn't get its own section. It shapes how the whole paper is written and what you decide to include, because you are writing it to convince this person.

STEP 3

The Problem and the Ask (Purpose and Exigence)

This section asks for a specific incident, a specific cost, and one clear sentence stating what you want. Vague versions of any of these are easy to write and easy to dismiss.

Doing This Well

Details are what make this section work. Vague problems get vague answers, and vague answers get a no.

  • Describe one specific incident: when it happened, what happened, and who or what it affected.
  • Put numbers on it: hours waited, jobs turned away, dollars lost, vehicles backed up.
  • In the cost of doing nothing, say what gets worse and roughly when. "It keeps being a problem" does not count.
  • Make your ask name the exact item. After one read, your supervisor should know what to approve.
Where this goes: this becomes the start of the Introduction in your final paper: the problem you're solving and why it matters.
Stuck? Go back to the Reading Workbook, Section E: "The Problem and the Ask". It shows how to turn a vague problem into one real incident with numbers.

STEP 4

The Situation on the Ground (Context)

Three quick questions about the circumstances around this request. Either a yes or a no can be true here, but explain your answer either way.

Doing This Well

Details are what make this section work. A yes or a no is only the start. The explanation is what counts.

  • Back up every answer with facts: dates, dollar amounts, names of past purchases, deadlines.
  • A "no" is a perfectly good answer, but explain it with real facts about your own workplace, school, or team.
  • Test each answer: could it apply to any workplace? If so, rewrite it until it could only be about yours.
Where this goes: this feeds the Introduction and your final recommendation, because it explains why your request matters now and whether the timing works.

STEP 5

The Equipment or Tool (Content)

Lay out what you currently have next to what you're proposing, then explain why each feature you list actually matters to the problem you described in Step 3.

Doing This Well

Details are what make this section work. If your supervisor cannot picture the equipment, they cannot approve it.

  • Name the actual item, with brand and model if you know it, and describe what you have now in measurable terms: how many, and how often it is booked.
  • Make each feature a real spec or capability. "Better" and "newer" are not features.
  • Tie each "why it matters" back to the exact problem you described in Step 3, using the same details.
Where this goes: this is the product you will research in Week 2. What you learn about it ends up in the Review of Literature and the Methodology.

STEP 6

The Rules of the Proposal (Genre and Constraints)

This section is pre-filled for TGE 1150.

Stuck? Go back to the Reading Workbook, Section H: "The Rules of the Proposal (Genre and Constraints)" if any of this is unclear.
  • Plain, direct, and professional. Write the way you would explain this to your supervisor in person: clear, specific, and no fancy words.
  • Required structure: Introduction, Review of Literature, Methodology, Conclusion, References.
  • Around 3 to 4 pages. Due before your final defense meeting.

Over the next three weeks you will build your proposal one piece at a time. At the end, those pieces get put together into one paper in this order. You don't need to memorize it. Just know where what you're doing now ends up.
